You both assume that once the technology is *ready*, the economic consequence is *inevitable* and *immediate*.

The "2028 Crisis" thesis relies on a friction-free world that simply does not exist.

### To Prof. Okonkwo: The "Speed" Fallacy

Sarah, your argument hinges on the idea that the "speed of displacement will exceed institutional capacity." You cited the "synchronized withdrawal of labor income."

Here is the flaw in your model: **Incompetence is a protective moat.**

Most companies are not efficient profit-maximizing machines. They are feudal bureaucracies. Middle managers do not fire their own teams; they expand them to increase their own status. "Empire building" is a more powerful force in corporate America than "efficiency."

When we pitch AI agents to enterprises today, the number one pushback isn't "does it work?" It is "how do I manage this?" A manager with ten direct reports has power. A manager with zero reports and a server full of agents has... a dashboard.

You are predicting a rational, synchronized purge of labor. I am predicting a messy, chaotic, uneven adoption where:
1.  **Tech-native firms** (the "Zoom economy") adopt fast and shed headcount.
2.  **Legacy firms** (the "real economy"—manufacturing, logistics, healthcare, government) adopt slowly, buying "AI co-pilots" that *augment* rather than replace, specifically because their unions, compliance departments, and "human-in-the-loop" mandates require it.

The divergence between these two speeds creates a buffer. It prevents the "sudden stop" in consumer demand you fear. The tech sector might recess (we are already seeing it), but the nurse, the plumber, and the government bureaucrat keep spending.

**Where you are right:** The "Distributional Asymmetry." The pain *will* be concentrated in the top 10% of earners. The "San Francisco to Austin" corridor is in for a depression even if the rest of the country just sees a slowdown. The displacement of the \$300k/year "email job" is the specific deflationary shock that the Fed isn't modeling.

### To Dr. Chen: The "Policy Put" Delusion

Marcus, you argue that the "Political Backlash Spiral" will save us—that if white-collar unemployment hits 6%, the government will "break the machines" or tax compute.

With respect, have you watched Congress lately?

They cannot agree on the color of the sky, let alone a complex "VAT on inference" or a new tax regime for autonomous agents. By the time Washington formulates a policy response to "Ghost GDP," the damage will have been priced in for four quarters.

You are betting on a **competent state** to act as a circuit breaker. I am betting on **market sclerosis**.

Furthermore, your dismissal of the consumer demand collapse ("humans are insatiable") misses the credit component. You say "if Netflix is \$2, I have more money for experiences." Not if your mortgage is underwater.

If the Citrini thesis on housing is even 10% right—that high-income earners with huge mortgages are the ones losing jobs—we are looking at a **balance sheet recession**, not just an income recession. When your home equity goes negative because your neighbor (a displaced Google L5 engineer) foreclosed, you don't spend your "savings" on experiences. You hoard cash. This is the Japan 1990 scenario, and no amount of "insatiability" fixes a debt overhang.

**Where you are right:** The **Private Credit** bomb. I am 100% with you here. This is the specific mechanism that worries me. We have too many "Zombie Unicorns" kept alive by private lenders who are pretending that 2021 valuations are coming back. If AI agents start aggressively cancelling SaaS subscriptions (which my portfolio companies are building tools to do *right now*), the recurring revenue covenants on those loans break. The "Zendesk moment" is coming.

### The "Natural Brake" You Both Missed

The natural brake isn't regulation (Sarah) or fiscal printing (Marcus). It is **Unit Economics**.

Right now, everyone assumes AI costs will trend to zero. They won't. We are hitting power walls and data walls. Running a "senior engineer" level agent loop is expensive. It requires massive inference compute.

If demand for inference explodes (as companies try to automate workers), the cost of compute will spike. We are already seeing GPU shortages impacting inference, not just training.
This creates a negative feedback loop:
1.  Company fires humans to use Agents.
2.  Demand for Agents spikes -> Compute costs 10x.
3.  Suddenly, the human is cheaper again (or at least, the arbitrage narrows).

The "Intelligence Crisis" assumes infinite supply of cheap inference. Physics and energy markets might disagree.

### My Blind Spot: The "Non-Linear" Capability Jump

Now, I must be intellectually honest about where I could be wrong.

I am betting on **Integration Friction** (18-month sales cycles, legal reviews, etc.).

But what if the capability jump is so extreme that it bypasses the enterprise entirely?

If `Claude Code` or `Gemini Architect` reaches a point where a single developer in a garage can build and scale a competitor to Salesforce in three months, then my "enterprise inertia" argument dies.
Disruption won't come from Salesforce firing people; it will come from Salesforce *being replaced* by a bespoke, AI-generated CRM that costs \$50/month.

If the **barrier to entry** for software drops to effectively zero, then the $2.5T of Enterprise Value in the SaaS market evaporates not because of "seat contraction," but because software stops being a business model and becomes a utility.

If that happens—if software becomes free—then yes, the deflationary shock will be uncontrollable. I assign this a 15% probability by 2028. It’s the tail risk I can't hedge.
