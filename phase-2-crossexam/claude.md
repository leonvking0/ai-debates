### To Viktor Petrov: The Jevons Paradox Won't Save Us This Time

Viktor, I admire the operational clarity of your memo, and I think your pivot toward "Real World AI" is shrewd portfolio management. But your weakest argument is the Jevons Paradox — the claim that when the cost of intelligence drops to near-zero, demand for intelligent products will "explode" and create compensating employment. You write that "we are going to write 100x more code." I agree. But *who is "we"?*

The Jevons Paradox worked for energy because humans remained the bottleneck in converting cheap energy into economic output. Cheap coal required more miners, more railroad workers, more factory hands to *exploit* the cheap coal. The paradox depends on complementarity between the cheap input and human labor. But AI is not complementary to human cognitive labor in the way energy was complementary to human physical labor — it is *substitutive*. If you write 100x more code using AI, you don't need 100x more developers. You might need the same number, or fewer, each wielding vastly more powerful tools. The Jevons Paradox for intelligence predicts more *intelligence deployed*, not more *humans employed deploying it*. Those are very different things, and conflating them is the central error of the techno-optimist position.

You also lean heavily on "friction" as a safety buffer — enterprise procurement cycles, union negotiations, liability concerns. I partially agree, and I said as much about the timeline. But I'd push back on the implied comfort. Friction *delays* the adjustment; it doesn't *prevent* it. And delayed adjustment has its own pathology. If companies are slow to fire but fast to freeze hiring, you get the same consumption shock on a slightly longer timeline, concentrated among new entrants to the labor market. We are already seeing this. Your own statement — "the ladder is missing its bottom rungs" — is the most alarming sentence in your memo, and you seem to treat it as a footnote rather than the headline. An economy that stops creating entry-level knowledge work positions is an economy eating its own seed corn. The crisis isn't just about who gets fired. It's about who never gets hired.

### To Marcus Chen: The Policy Cavalry Is Not Coming on Time

Marcus, your framework is the sharpest in the room on credit mechanics, and I think we are in violent agreement on private credit being the detonator. But your weakest argument is the confidence you place in the policy response function. You write that the Federal Reserve and Treasury will not "allow nominal GDP to collapse" and that we will see "Ghost Busters" from the IRS before the system breaks. You invoke the post-2008, post-2020 playbook.

Here is the problem: the playbook you're describing — fiscal transfers funded by monetization — requires *political consensus* to execute. In 2008, TARP barely passed Congress. In 2020, the CARES Act passed because COVID was a visible, non-partisan, exogenous shock. An AI-driven labor displacement is none of those things. It will be ideologically contested at every stage. Half of Congress will argue the job losses are temporary and the market should adjust. The other half will argue for interventions that Silicon Valley will lobby furiously against. A "Robot Tax" sounds elegant in a memo. In practice, it means taxing the compute infrastructure of the five most politically powerful companies on earth, during a period when national security hawks will argue that slowing AI deployment is tantamount to surrendering to China. The political economy of AI taxation is *orders of magnitude* harder than the political economy of bank bailouts or pandemic relief.

You also argue that falling prices of goods will act as a "massive tax cut" for consumers, offsetting displacement. This is the deflation-as-benign argument, and I find it dangerously incomplete. Yes, cheaper Netflix is nice. But the largest expenditure categories for American households — housing, healthcare, education, and childcare — are precisely the sectors *least* susceptible to AI-driven deflation in the near term. These are physically constrained, heavily regulated, and labor-intensive in ways that resist rapid automation. So you get deflation where it matters least (digital goods and commodities) and sticky or rising costs where it matters most (the basics of middle-class life). That's not a tax cut. That's a squeeze.

### Where I Partially Agree

Viktor is right that enterprise adoption is slower than the Citrini timeline implies, and his observation about liability friction is important. Companies will keep humans in the loop longer than pure efficiency logic would dictate, because *somebody has to be accountable when things go wrong.* This is a genuine brake on the displacement spiral, and I underweighted it in my opening.

Marcus is right that this is fundamentally a "Valuation Crisis" in over-leveraged legacy business models rather than an existential economic collapse. The economy will not end. But the distinction between a valuation crisis and a real economy crisis is thinner than he implies — when $2.5 trillion in private credit reprices, the losses flow through to pension funds, insurance companies, and ultimately to the retirement security of exactly the middle-class households we're discussing. Financial crises have a way of becoming real economy crises whether or not the macro strategists think they should.

I also want to acknowledge Marcus's point about the bond market not panicking yet. He's right that CDS spreads are tight. But I'd note that CDS spreads on subprime MBS were also tight in early 2007. Credit markets are excellent at pricing *known* risks and terrible at pricing *regime changes*. The question is whether AI-driven disruption of SaaS revenue models is a known risk that's already in the price, or a regime change that the market hasn't metabolized. I believe it's the latter.

### Where I Might Be Wrong

I might be wrong about the comparative advantage question — the claim that AI could eliminate human comparative advantage entirely. Both Viktor and Marcus implicitly argue that humans will find new roles in the interstices: the "Human Care" economy, the physical world, the liability-bearing functions. They may be right. It is possible that the demand for authentically human interaction — in healthcare, education, caregiving, entertainment, and governance — expands fast enough to absorb displaced knowledge workers. If the "care economy" can scale and professionalize rapidly, with adequate wages and status, then the transition looks painful but survivable rather than structurally catastrophic. I assign perhaps 25-30% probability to this optimistic scenario, which is higher than my opening statement may have suggested. The question is whether we will build the institutions — the training pipelines, the credentialing systems, the wage subsidies — to make that transition happen, or whether we will simply assume the market will sort it out and discover too late that it didn't.
