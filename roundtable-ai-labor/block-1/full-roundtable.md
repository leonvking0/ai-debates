## 1. The Demand Problem: Real, But Not the Way Most People Frame It

The demand problem is real in the medium term (3-10 years) but likely self-correcting in the long term (10-25 years) — and the correction mechanism is political, not economic. That distinction matters enormously for portfolio construction.

Say's Law doesn't "break." It was never a law. It's an identity that holds in aggregate over sufficient time horizons but tells you nothing about the transition path. The transition path is where people lose their homes, where populists get elected, and where my clients' assets get taxed or expropriated. I don't have the luxury of waiting for the long run.

Here's my base case with rough probabilities:

- **Most likely (55%):** AI displacement is gradual enough that labor markets adjust messily but without systemic collapse. Real wages stagnate or decline for the bottom 40-60% of cognitive workers over 10-15 years, similar to what happened to manufacturing workers from 1980-2010. Consumer spending shifts composition but doesn't collapse because government transfer payments expand significantly. Think: augmented welfare state, funded by some combination of deficit spending and capital taxation.
- **Plausible downside (30%):** Displacement is faster than institutional adaptation. We get a 2-5 year window where unemployment spikes above 8-10% in developed economies, consumer confidence craters, and we see a demand-driven recession that monetary policy can't easily fix because the problem is structural, not cyclical. This is the scenario where equities draw down 30-45%.
- **Tail risk (15%):** A genuine deflationary spiral where AI productivity gains accrue almost entirely to capital, consumer demand collapses, and the political system fails to redistribute fast enough. This is the 1930s analogy. I assign it low probability not because the economics are impossible but because modern central banks and fiscal authorities have the tools to prevent it — the question is whether they have the political will.

**Confidence level: Medium.** My biggest uncertainty is speed of displacement. If someone showed me convincing evidence that AI could replace 30%+ of cognitive tasks within 3 years rather than 10, I'd shift significant probability mass from scenario one to scenario two.

## 3. AI-Consuming-Resources as Demand Substitute: Mostly Circular

I've heard this argument from several tech-sector allocators and I'm not persuaded. The claim that AI spending on compute, energy, and data constitutes genuine final demand confuses two things: intermediate inputs and final consumption.

NVIDIA selling $80B+ in GPUs annually is real economic activity. Data center construction is real. Energy demand is real. But these are all *derived demand* — derived from an expectation that AI outputs will eventually generate value for human end-users or replace costs currently borne by humans. If the terminal human use case shrinks — because humans have less income to spend on AI-enhanced goods and services — then the derived demand eventually shrinks too. You can't bootstrap an economy on machines buying things from other machines indefinitely. At some point the chain of derived demand has to terminate in something a human or a government values enough to pay for.

The exception — and I allocate around this — is if governments become the buyer of last resort for AI services. National security applications, public health, infrastructure management, climate adaptation. Government spending as 35-40% of GDP (up from 24% in the U.S. currently) is plausible in an AI-abundant economy and would provide a genuine demand floor.

**Portfolio implication:** I'm overweight companies that serve government and regulated-industry AI demand (defense tech, healthcare AI, infrastructure) versus pure consumer-facing AI plays, precisely because the government demand channel is more durable than the consumer channel in a displacement scenario.

**Confidence level: Medium-high.** I'd revise this if AI systems began exhibiting genuine autonomous economic agency — making purchasing decisions, accumulating resources, pursuing goals that create real economic cycles independent of human utility. I think that's a 5-10 year question, not a current one, and it raises issues well beyond economics.

## 4. The "No Up to Move To" Problem: This Is Where I Have Lowest Confidence

The historical analogies are weaker than people want to believe, and I say this as someone who has used them in investor letters.

The agricultural-to-industrial transition worked because factory work required *different* skills, not *higher* skills. A farmer could become a factory worker in weeks. The industrial-to-service transition worked because service work required interpersonal and cognitive skills that humans inherently possessed. In both cases, the new jobs were *complementary* to human capabilities.

The AI transition is different in kind because it targets the cognitive and analytical skills that were the "move up" destination in every prior transition. If AI can do legal analysis, medical diagnosis, financial modeling, software engineering, and creative work — what is the next rung on the ladder? The honest answer is: I don't know, and neither does anyone else.

Three scenarios I'm modeling:

- **"New capabilities" (40%):** Humans develop roles we can't currently envision, just as "social media manager" was inconceivable in 1990. The economy creates demand for human judgment, taste, physical presence, and relational skills at scale. Growth continues, inequality widens but is manageable.
- **"Permanent bifurcation" (35%):** A smaller elite of AI-complementary workers (those who direct, curate, and govern AI systems) earn very well. The majority of the population works in low-productivity personal services or relies on transfers. Think: a much richer version of the current gig economy. GDP grows, median living standards stagnate.
- **"True displacement" (25%):** AI capabilities advance fast enough that even the complementary roles shrink. We're in genuinely unprecedented territory requiring a fundamental rethinking of income distribution. UBI or equivalent becomes a necessity, not a policy choice.

**What I'd need to change my position:** Evidence that AI capability curves are flattening — that there are hard limits to what LLMs and successor architectures can do that leave large categories of cognitive work exclusively human. The trend data over the past 3 years has moved in the opposite direction, which is why I've been steadily increasing the probability I assign to scenarios two and three.

## gemini Opening

In addressing these fundamental questions, I begin with the standard neoclassical production function: $Y = A \cdot f(K, L)$. Our primary concern is the evolution of $A$ (Total Factor Productivity) and the distributional parameters that dictate how $Y$ is partitioned between $K$ (capital) and $L$ (labor). While I acknowledge the potential for significant structural friction, my baseline remains grounded in the long-run equilibrium properties of growth theory.

### 1. The "Demand Problem" and the Persistence of Say’s Law

The fear of a deflationary demand spiral due to AI-driven labor displacement often conflates nominal wage suppression with a collapse in real aggregate demand. From a growth accounting perspective, if AI raises TFP ($A$) significantly—projections from Goldman Sachs and others suggest a potential 1.5 percentage point increase in annual GDP growth over a decade—the cost of production for goods and services must fall.

Say’s Law, in its modern interpretation, suggests that the income generated by this increased production does not vanish; it is redistributed. If AI replaces a paralegal, the firm’s costs drop. Under competitive pressures, this results in lower prices for legal services, which increases the real disposable income of all consumers of those services. This is the "real balance effect" or the Pigou effect. The "demand problem" only becomes a terminal "demand collapse" if we assume a total failure of price adjustment or an infinite liquidity preference (a permanent liquidity trap). 

I estimate the probability of a permanent, AI-induced deflationary spiral to be a **tail risk (<10%)**. The more likely scenario (**>70%**) is a period of "growth deflation" similar to the late 19th century, where TFP gains drove prices down while real output expanded. My highest confidence lies in the fact that human wants are non-satiated; as long as marginal utility remains positive for *some* good or service, the income liberated from lower-cost production will seek new outlets.

### 2. Wealth Concentration and the MPC Tipping Point

This is the area where my framework faces the greatest challenge. The optimistic case for AD (Aggregate Demand) relies on the recycling of capital income into investment or consumption. However, the Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC) out of capital income is significantly lower (historically ~0.03 to 0.05) than the MPC out of labor income (~0.6 to 0.9 for lower-decile earners).

If the labor share of income—which has already declined from roughly 65% in the mid-20th century to approximately 58% today—were to collapse to, say, 30%, we face a structural "oversaving" problem. Mathematically, the tipping point occurs when the required investment to sustain $Y$ exceeds the profitable opportunities for that capital, leading to secular stagnation. 

Using a simplified Harrod-Domar framework, if the capital-to-output ratio ($v$) is 3, and the savings rate ($s$) jumps from 20% to 40% due to wealth concentration, the economy requires a warranted growth rate of 13.3% to clear the market. This is historically unprecedented. I view a "stagnationary crisis" driven by wealth concentration as a **plausible scenario (30-40%)**. We would likely see a transition from a labor-based economy to one that necessitates massive fiscal transfers (e.g., UBI) to maintain the velocity of money, essentially "re-priming" the MPC via the state.

### 3. Derived Demand vs. Final Demand: The "Compute" Fallacy

There is a burgeoning argument that AI’s demand for energy, silicon, and data will replace human consumption. From a National Income Accounting perspective, this is a category error. 

Energy and compute are **intermediate goods**. They are inputs into the production process, not final consumption ($C$). If AI "consumes" 20% of global electricity to produce better diagnostic software, that electricity is a cost of production. The *final demand* remains the human patient who wants the diagnosis. 

If the "terminal human use case" shrinks—i.e., if humans lack the income to buy the products AI is making—then the demand for compute itself must eventually collapse. You cannot have a sustainable economy where robots build data centers to power robots to build more data centers, unless those data centers are producing a service that eventually terminates in human utility or a meaningful capital investment. A "circular AI economy" that excludes human utility is not an economy; it is a thermodynamic process. I am **highly confident (>90%)** that AI resource consumption cannot substitute for final demand. Without a link to human utility, the "AI boom" would be a classic speculative bubble in the capital goods sector, akin to the overbuilding of fiber optics in the late 1990s, but on a much larger scale.

### 4. Historical Analogies and the "No Upward Mobility" Problem

We often cite the transition from horses to cars or the shift from agriculture to manufacturing. In 1900, 41% of the US workforce was in agriculture; by 2000, it was 2%. The "upward" move was from manual brawn to cognitive skill. 

The current disruption is qualitatively different because it targets the **cognitive ceiling**. If we model labor as a set of tasks (the Acemoglu-Autor framework), AI is moving from "routine manual" to "non-routine cognitive" tasks. If the "comparative advantage" of humans is pushed into a shrinking corner of "empathy-based" or "physical-presence" tasks, we face two risks:
1.  **The "Lump of Labor" might actually be real this time:** If the elasticity of substitution between AI and human labor is greater than 1 across all high-value sectors.
2.  **Wage Polarization:** Even if employment stays high, the real wage for "human-only" tasks may fall below subsistence levels without intervention.

Historical analogies remain valid for the *process* of creative destruction, but they may fail to predict the *destination*. I am **uncertain (confidence <50%)** on whether new sectors will emerge to absorb displaced cognitive labor. While the 1990s created "Web Developers," those were still cognitive tasks. If AI occupies the cognitive space, the next "up" might be "Bio-hybridization" or something outside current economic modeling.

### Summary of Position

I believe the "demand problem" is primarily a **distributional and transition problem**, not a fundamental failure of the production-income loop. 

*   **Likely (>70%):** AI significantly boosts TFP, leading to lower prices and higher aggregate wealth, but with severe structural unemployment in the middle-skill cognitive bracket.
*   **Plausible (30-40%):** A "capital-bias" crisis where the MPC of capital owners is too low to sustain aggregate demand, necessitating a fundamental rewrite of the social contract (fiscal intervention).
*   **Tail Risk (<10%):** A true "end of work" where human labor has zero marginal product, leading to a collapse of the current market-based exchange system.

My challenge to the other participants: If you argue for a demand collapse, you must specify why the price mechanism (deflation) fails to restore real purchasing power, or why capital owners will refuse to either consume or invest their gains. Show me the math on where the income "disappears."

## codex Opening

My base case: the demand problem is real in transition, not inevitable in steady state. Capability is compounding fast; institutions and distribution mechanisms are not. That mismatch is where most economic pain will come from in the next 10 years.

**1) Deflationary demand spiral: real risk or category error?**

The risk is real, but conditional. If AI cuts cognitive labor hours per unit output by 50-80% in large sectors while wage-setting institutions remain unchanged, you get a demand gap before you get a new equilibrium.

Say’s Law is not a useful short-run guide in this regime. In modern monetary economies, supply capacity can rise while purchasing power is concentrated in balance sheets with low consumption propensity. So you can have rising productive potential and weak realized demand simultaneously.

I’d frame probabilities this way (next 10 years, OECD-heavy view):
1. Likely (>50%): recurring demand shortfalls in exposed sectors (media, software services, customer support, back-office functions), offset by policy and deficit spending enough to avoid full deflationary spiral.
2. Plausible (10-50%): economy-wide “slow-burn disinflation + stagnant median incomes” despite measured GDP/productivity gains.
3. Tail risk (<10%): true generalized debt-deflation spiral across advanced economies. Central banks and fiscal authorities are too interventionist now for a 1930s replay unless there is policy paralysis.

What prevents collapse is not automatic market clearing; it is institutional response: transfers, fiscal deficits, public procurement, labor market frictions, and sectoral bottlenecks (housing, health, energy infrastructure) that keep nominal spending channels active.

Where I’d change my view: if by ~2032 we see labor share fall sharply (say >10 percentage points) without any sustained rise in transfers or public investment, I’d raise deflation risk materially.

**2) Capital income substitution and MPC tipping points**

This is where most optimistic narratives get hand-wavy. You need arithmetic, not vibes.

Use a stripped model:  
\( C = c_L \cdot Y_L + c_K \cdot Y_K + C_0 \)  
where \( c_L \) (MPC from labor income) is high and \( c_K \) (MPC from capital income) is lower.

Reasonable directional values: \( c_L \approx 0.8-0.95 \), \( c_K \approx 0.2-0.5 \) depending on distribution and credit conditions. If income shifts from labor to concentrated capital claims, aggregate consumption demand weakens unless one of these offsets occurs:
1. redistribution/transfers,
2. higher government dissaving,
3. lower real rates inducing private investment booms,
4. export absorption.

A functional tipping zone appears when effective MPC drops below what is needed to absorb output at full employment. I’d loosely locate this around aggregate MPC <0.55-0.60 in advanced economies. You don’t get instant collapse; you get chronic slack, asset inflation, and political stress.

Concentration threshold: not one magic number, but if top decile captures a very large majority of AI rents while median wage income is flat-to-down, demand fragility becomes structural. If that persists for 5+ years, policy response becomes less optional and more forced.

My confidence here is high on direction, medium on exact thresholds. Economics is regime-dependent; fiscal rules, debt tolerance, and global capital flows matter as much as MPC parameters.

What would falsify my concern: evidence that AI-driven capital income is broadly diffused (pensions, sovereign funds, broad equity ownership, labor-linked profit sharing) enough to keep effective MPC stable.

**3) Can AI-to-AI demand replace human demand?**

This is my most controversial view, and it needs precision.

AI-to-AI activity is economically real as intermediate demand. If one agent buys inference, data labeling, simulation, or code-generation services from another, that is production and income flow. It can sustain investment cycles for a while, especially in compute, energy, and network infrastructure.

But intermediate demand is not self-justifying forever. Derived demand needs terminal value somewhere: human consumption, public goods, strategic state objectives, or long-horizon capital formation with eventual use.

So the strong claim “AI can fully replace human demand” is mostly circular today. The weaker claim “AI-to-AI demand can materially buffer transition shocks and become a large GDP share” is plausible.

My split:
1. Likely (>50%): AI-to-AI transactions become a major intermediate layer in software, analytics, logistics, and R&D workflows.
2. Plausible (10-50%): this layer temporarily masks weak household demand by driving capex booms in compute/energy.
3. Tail risk (<10%): a mostly closed AI economy with minimal human terminal demand and stable growth. That requires non-human terminal objectives at scale (e.g., autonomous space industry, large autonomous public works), which we’re far from.

If terminal human use cases shrink and policy does nothing, the AI-to-AI loop eventually hits overcapacity: falling returns on compute, consolidation, debt stress in infrastructure-heavy firms.

Where I’d revise upward: if autonomous systems start reliably converting AI activity into physical capital with independent strategic value (energy grids, materials discovery, automated manufacturing) at scale.

**4) Are historical analogies valid when cognitive “upskilling ladders” are attacked?**

Analogies are partially valid on diffusion and institutional lag; invalid on labor substitution pattern.

Valid part: general-purpose technologies diffuse slower than capability hype suggests. Firms reorganize slowly. Complementary assets (process redesign, regulation, workforce systems) dominate timelines. That argues against overnight mass unemployment.

Invalid part: previous waves often displaced routine physical labor while creating broad cognitive supervisory/clerical ladders. This wave directly compresses mid-skill cognitive tasks. The “move up” pathway narrows because AI also climbs the ladder.

My base forecast for 5-10 years:
1. Likely (>50%): major task automation (not full-job automation) across knowledge work; 30-60% time savings in many roles, 50-80% in specific workflows.
2. Likely (>50%): labor hours per unit output fall much faster than headcount initially, because firms expand output and defer layoffs where demand exists.
3. Plausible (10-50%): by mid-2030s, full automation risk becomes high for specific job families (tier-1 support, routine content production, standardized analysis), while new human roles are fewer and more elite.
4. Tail risk (<10%): “no up at all” world where almost all non-manual workers are economically redundant within a decade. Institutions and complementarity effects make this too extreme near-term.

So, “no up” is too strong; “up exists but is narrower, steeper, and less inclusive” is the better model.

What changes my position: if we observe sustained net job creation in new cognitive categories large enough to absorb displaced hours without wage compression, I’d downgrade disruption severity. If instead we see persistent underemployment among college-educated workers plus flat real wages despite productivity gains, I’d upgrade structural risk.

Highest confidence: capability-adoption gap, task-level restructuring, and distribution-driven demand fragility.  
Lowest confidence: exact MPC tipping thresholds and long-run share of AI-to-AI activity that can persist without explicit redistributive policy or new terminal demand domains.

### 3. Defense of the Equilibrium: The Price Mechanism

Both of you express skepticism toward Say’s Law and the price mechanism. Victoria says, "People lose their homes in the transition." Codex says, "Supply capacity can rise while purchasing power is concentrated."

I do not dispute the "transition pain." I dispute the idea that this pain represents a terminal failure of demand. If AI makes the cost of a "basket of goods" fall by 50%, and nominal wages also fall by 50%, real purchasing power is unchanged. The "demand collapse" only happens if:
1.  **Prices are sticky:** (e.g., housing or energy don't fall as fast as wages).
2.  **Debt is nominal:** (The Fisherian debt-deflation spiral).

If Victoria’s clients hold debt over the displaced workers, then deflation *is* a disaster because the real value of that debt explodes. But this is a **financial architecture problem**, not a "demand problem" inherent to AI. If we are truly entering an era of 5-10% TFP growth, we should expect—and welcome—secular deflation. The problem is that our financial system is built on the assumption of 2% inflation. We are blaming the technology for the rigidities of our contracts.

### 4. Nuance: The Elasticity of Substitution

I agree with Victoria that the "no up to move to" problem is the greatest unknown. However, I think you both miss the crucial nuance of the **Elasticity of Substitution ($\sigma$)**.

In most of the 20th century, we assumed $\sigma = 1$ (Cobb-Douglas), meaning labor and capital shares stay constant even as technology improves. If AI makes $\sigma > 1$, then any improvement in AI *automatically* reduces the labor share of income. This is the "Zeira Model" of automation. If $\sigma$ is high enough, the labor share doesn't just "stagnate"—it trends to zero. 

If this is the case, then Codex's "task-level" analysis is too optimistic. It doesn't matter if some tasks remain human; if the *price* of the tasks AI can do falls to near-zero, the "comparative advantage" of humans in empathy or physical presence won't save their wages. They will be like the "human computers" of the 1940s—perfectly capable of the task, but economically irrelevant because the machine cost is $10^{-6}$ of the human cost.

### 5. Where I Might Be Wrong: The "End of Non-Satiation"

My entire framework rests on the assumption of **non-satiety**: that humans will always want *more* or *different* things. If we reach a point where humans are "satisfied" with the goods and services AI provides, and they have no desire for new human-produced services, then the "new sectors" I predict will never emerge.

If marginal utility ($MU$) hits zero for the median human, then Say's Law truly breaks. In that world, the economy stops being a system of exchange and becomes a system of distribution. I assign this a **low probability (<15%)** over the next 20 years, but if it happens, my neoclassical models are useless. We would be in a "post-scarcity" regime where "demand" is a psychological category, not an economic one.

### Summary Challenge to the Panel:

*   **To Victoria:** If you believe in a "permanent bifurcation" where the elite directs AI and the rest rely on transfers, show me the **dynamic stability** of that model. Does the "elite" have enough $MU$ for human-led services to keep the velocity of money from hitting zero?
*   **To Codex:** You mention "autonomous space industry" or "public works" as non-human terminal demand. Is that demand, or is that just a **state-funded science project**? If the latter, we are back to Victoria’s "government as buyer of last resort" problem. Who pays for the rocket if no one is buying the satellite's data?

I remain convinced that TFP is the only thing that matters in the long run. If $Y$ grows, the "problem" is a mere matter of who owns the ledger. We are arguing over the plumbing of a house that is growing ten times larger. Let us not confuse a wet floor with a structural collapse.

## codex Cross-Examination

Both of you are directionally right on parts of the problem, but each of you has one weak link that breaks your full conclusion.

**1. Weakest argument from Victoria (Claude): “Government as buyer of last resort solves the demand floor.”**

Your weakest claim is not that politics matters. It does. Your weak claim is that this can be relied on as a macro stabilizer fast enough, and at the scale needed, to absorb AI-driven labor-income shocks.

The arithmetic is harsher than your framing implies. If AI-driven reorganization cuts effective cognitive labor demand by even 10% of total payroll over 5 years in a large economy, that is a multi-trillion-dollar annual income reallocation in the US context. To keep aggregate demand stable, fiscal response must show up quickly as high-MPC transfers, not just procurement contracts. Defense and infrastructure AI spend has long lead times, concentrated recipients, and lower immediate household multiplier than direct transfers.

So yes, government can be the floor, but only under specific implementation conditions:
1. Automatic stabilizers expand without new legislative cycles.
2. Transfer design targets liquidity-constrained households.
3. Tax/transfer plumbing updates on 12-24 month timelines, not 5-10 years.

Without those, your “most likely 55% messy adjustment” drifts toward your own downside scenario. I think you underweight state-capacity friction relative to financial-market reaction speed.

**2. Weakest argument from Gemini: “Price adjustment will mostly rescue demand via real purchasing power.”**

Your weakest argument is the strong reliance on the Pigou-style channel in a balance-sheet economy with nominal debt, sticky sectors, and labor market segmentation.

Deflation in tradable digital services does not automatically offset income loss in non-tradables that dominate household budgets. Housing, healthcare, education, childcare, insurance, and local services are where households feel constraint. If displaced white-collar workers lose wage income, cheaper software does not clear rent.

Three concrete frictions you understate:
1. Debt-deflation dynamics: falling prices can raise real debt burdens and depress consumption further.
2. Sectoral CPI mismatch: AI cuts prices where budget share is often smaller for constrained households.
3. Labor income uncertainty: precautionary savings rises when employment volatility rises, lowering effective MPC.

So your <10% tail-risk on deflationary spiral may be right for a 1930s-style collapse, but your >70% confidence in benign growth-deflation is too high. I’d put benign transition lower unless institutional redistribution is pre-committed.

**3. Defense of my position where you pushed back**

My core claim stands: demand risk is transitional and distributional, not proof of imminent economic extinction.

Where I disagree with Gemini: the market-clearing story is too smooth for institutional reality. Capability can jump on 6-18 month cycles; enterprise redesign, labor reallocation, and fiscal adaptation often take 3-7 years. That lag is the macro risk.

Where I disagree with Victoria: AI-to-AI demand is not pure circular nonsense in the medium term. It can act as a real capex and productivity buffer. If autonomous agents raise R&D throughput, code velocity, and infrastructure utilization, that creates investable cash flows and measured GDP contributions for a while. But it is still mostly intermediate demand unless anchored in final demand: household utility, state objectives, or long-horizon strategic capital formation.

So the right view is neither “self-sustaining machine economy now” nor “AI capex is fake.” It is a transitional supercycle with endpoint uncertainty.

My updated distribution for advanced economies over 10 years:
1. Likely (>50%): persistent sectoral demand shortfalls plus policy patchwork, no systemic collapse.
2. Plausible (10-50%): repeated recessions tied to labor-income shocks and delayed redistribution.
3. Tail risk (<10%): generalized debt-deflation spiral requiring extraordinary fiscal-monetary coordination.

**4. Partial agreements with missing nuance**

I agree with Victoria that the long-run correction is political. Missing nuance: it is less about ideology and more about administrative throughput. Countries with automatic tax withholding updates, portable benefits rails, and real-time labor market data will outperform regardless of party label.

I agree with Gemini that there is no single concentration “magic number.” Missing nuance: the operative variable is effective aggregate MPC after credit conditions and perceived income risk, not static MPC by decile. Fear can drop MPC fast even without large immediate income shifts.

Both of you underweight two structural nuances:
1. Hours vs headcount: firms can cut labor hours per unit output dramatically while keeping headline employment deceptively stable for years.
2. Externalization channel: leading AI economies can export surplus supply and temporarily outsource demand weakness to trade partners, delaying domestic adjustment while increasing geopolitical imbalance.

**5. One thing I might be wrong about**

I may be underestimating how quickly capital income can be socially diffused without full redistribution politics. If pension systems, sovereign funds, broad-index ownership, and profit-sharing rails capture a meaningful share of AI rents, effective household MPC may hold up better than my base case expects.

1. By 2030, labor share falls but median real consumption still rises steadily without major transfer expansion.
2. Broad household balance-sheet exposure to AI rents increases measurably, not just top-decile wealth.
3. We observe sustained net job creation in new AI-complementary categories at wage levels that absorb displaced cognitive hours.

Bottom line: the demand problem is real, but it is a governance-timing problem layered on top of a capability shock, not a theorem that markets automatically solve or an automatic path to collapse.
