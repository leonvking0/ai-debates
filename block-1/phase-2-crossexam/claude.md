## Challenging Codex: The MPC Model Is Too Clean

Codex, I appreciate the formal consumption function — it's the right instinct to put math behind this. But your model assumes stable coefficients in a regime that, by definition, is unstable.

You write $C = c_L \cdot Y_L + c_K \cdot Y_K + C_0$ and assign $c_L \approx 0.8\text{-}0.95$, $c_K \approx 0.2\text{-}0.5$. My problem: $c_K$ at 0.5 is doing enormous work in your model and I don't think it's empirically justified for the concentration levels we're discussing. The 0.5 figure is plausible for a broad middle class with 401(k)s and pension income. For the top 0.1% — the people who will capture disproportionate AI rents — $c_K$ is closer to 0.03-0.05. You're averaging across a distribution where the average is meaningless because the variance is the whole story.

Run your own model with $c_K = 0.05$ for the top decile capturing 70%+ of incremental capital income. Your "tipping zone" of aggregate MPC below 0.55-0.60 arrives much faster than your timeline suggests. I think you're right on direction but dangerously optimistic on timing.

Where I partially agree: your point that "capability-adoption gap" dominates near-term outcomes is exactly right and underappreciated. Most of my families' portfolio companies are struggling to *implement* AI, not being *destroyed* by it. The gap between what AI can do in a demo and what it does inside a Fortune 500 org is 3-5 years minimum. That buys time. But it doesn't buy a solution.

## Challenging Gemini: The Pigou Effect Is Not Your Friend

Gemini, your opening relies heavily on the price mechanism restoring real purchasing power — the "growth deflation" of the late 19th century as your >70% base case. I need to push back hard here because I've seen this argument lose people real money.

The late 19th century "growth deflation" analogy is weaker than it appears. Yes, prices fell while output expanded. But that period also featured the Long Depression (1873-1896), massive labor unrest (Haymarket, Pullman Strike, Homestead Strike), the rise of populist movements, and ultimately the Progressive Era that fundamentally restructured the relationship between capital and the state. If that's your base case, you should be *more* alarmed, not less. The real returns on capital during that period were extremely volatile, and several of my families' historical equivalents — Gilded Age fortunes — were substantially diminished by the political response.

More specifically: the Pigou effect requires that prices actually fall and that nominal debts can be serviced during the adjustment. In a modern economy with $35 trillion in federal debt, $13 trillion in household debt, and financial assets priced at 200%+ of GDP, broad deflation is catastrophic for balance sheets *before* it restores real purchasing power. You can't hand-wave the debt-deflation dynamic by citing the Pigou effect. Irving Fisher showed us why in 1933. Your model assumes a frictionless price adjustment that has never existed in a financialized economy.

Your challenge to us — "show me where the income disappears" — has a straightforward answer: it doesn't disappear, it *concentrates* and then gets saved, not spent. The income shows up in asset prices, not in consumer demand. We've been watching this happen in real time for 15 years: record corporate profits, record equity valuations, stagnant median wages, weak consumer spending growth. AI accelerates a dynamic that's already in motion.

Where I partially agree: your point on human wants being non-satiated is theoretically correct and practically important. People will always want things. The question is whether they can *pay* for them. Non-satiation of wants plus inability to pay equals political instability, not equilibrium.

## Defending My Position

Both of you implicitly suggest my probability assignments are roughly right but push back on different elements. Let me defend two points where I think I'm being more realistic than either of you.

**First, on government as buyer of last resort.** I assigned significant probability to government spending reaching 35-40% of GDP. Codex acknowledges this as part of the "institutional response" but doesn't center it. Gemini mentions fiscal transfers but treats them as a secondary mechanism. I think government spending *is* the mechanism, and the investment question is really "which governments will do this effectively?" That's why I'm allocated to jurisdictions — Singapore, Switzerland, Gulf states — where fiscal capacity and political will are most likely to align. The U.S. political system, with its structural gridlock, is among the *worst* positioned to execute the fiscal response that both of you assume will happen. That's a risk neither of you is pricing.

**Second, on the speed question.** My opening stated that if AI replaces 30%+ of cognitive tasks within 3 years rather than 10, I'd shift probability mass toward my downside scenario. Since we had this conversation, I've been watching the deployment data. Enterprise AI adoption is accelerating faster than my 2024 estimates predicted. I'm now closer to 40% probability on my "plausible downside" scenario, up from 30% a year ago. Neither of you seems to be updating on speed as aggressively as the evidence warrants.

## Where I Partially Agree But See Missing Nuance

Codex's point that "up exists but is narrower, steeper, and less inclusive" is the best framing I've heard. But the investment implication is underspecified. "Narrower and steeper" means the *variance* of human capital returns increases dramatically. Some workers become enormously more productive with AI; most don't. This implies that equity returns will also bifurcate — companies that successfully implement AI will pull away, while the long tail of firms that can't will stagnate. Index investing becomes a worse strategy. Active management — specifically, identifying which firms are genuinely AI-productive versus AI-performative — becomes more valuable. That's a portfolio construction insight neither of you drew out.

Gemini's Acemoglu-Autor task framework is the right lens, but I'd push further: the framework assumes tasks are *separable*. What if AI doesn't just automate individual tasks but automates the *integration* of tasks — the management function itself? That's the scenario where even "AI-complementary" roles shrink faster than expected, because the coordination layer that justified high human wages gets automated.

## Where I Might Be Wrong

I may be overweighting jurisdictional diversification as a hedge. My framework assumes that different political jurisdictions will respond differently to AI disruption, creating arbitrage opportunities. But if AI disruption is truly global and simultaneous, the political responses may converge — toward capital controls, wealth taxes, and digital asset regulation — simultaneously across jurisdictions. In that world, there's nowhere to run, and my families' geographic optionality is worth less than I'm pricing it. The correlation of political risk across jurisdictions in an AI-disrupted world could be much higher than historical patterns suggest, precisely because this is a global technology with global distributional consequences.

That's the scenario that keeps me up at night, because it's the one where my entire professional value proposition — "I can protect your wealth across regimes" — might simply be wrong.
