## I. What I Changed My Mind About

This debate moved me on two points, and I want to be precise about how.

**First, on the natural brakes.** Viktor's argument about compute costs as a negative feedback loop is genuinely important, and I had underweighted it. If inference demand spikes as every company tries to automate simultaneously, GPU scarcity and energy constraints create a price signal that slows adoption. This is not a permanent brake — costs will fall over time — but it is a *timing* brake, and timing is everything in my framework. The displacement spiral requires cheap, abundant inference to sustain itself. If compute costs spike during peak adoption, some companies will pause, and the synchronized withdrawal of labor income becomes staggered rather than simultaneous. That staggering buys institutional absorption time. I've revised my estimate of the "full cascade" timeline from five-to-eight years to something more like four-to-seven, with compute bottlenecks being the primary moderating variable.

**Second, on the policy response.** Marcus pushed me harder than I expected on this. I still believe political consensus for an AI-specific fiscal response will be agonizingly slow. But he's right that the *monetary* response function is faster and less politically constrained. The Fed doesn't need Congressional approval to open emergency lending facilities. If private credit markets seize, the Fed will intervene to prevent contagion — not because they've solved the underlying labor displacement problem, but because that's what central banks do. They buy time. I now think the probability of a full financial contagion event — the 57% drawdown, insurance balance sheet crisis, mortgage meltdown chain — is lower than the Citrini memo suggests. Perhaps 15-20% rather than the implicit 40-50% the article seems to assign. The monetary fire brigade will arrive. The structural fire in the labor market, however, will keep burning.

## II. Where I Remain Most Confident

I remain most confident in three claims, and the debate has only strengthened them.

**The distributional concentration of pain is the story.** Viktor essentially conceded this point when he wrote that the "San Francisco to Austin corridor is in for a depression." Marcus conceded it when he described the "Super-Prime to Subprime" borrower transition. Both are describing the same phenomenon I identified: the American consumption economy is structurally dependent on high-income knowledge workers, and those workers are disproportionately exposed to AI displacement. This is not a footnote. This is the macro thesis. A targeted depression in the top decile *is* a consumption crisis for the whole economy, because that decile drives the spending.

**The entry-level hiring freeze is already catastrophic and being ignored.** Viktor called it "the ladder missing its bottom rungs" and then moved on. Marcus didn't address it at all. This is the single most important leading indicator we have. If companies stop hiring juniors because AI handles entry-level cognitive work, you don't just get unemployment — you get a *permanent skills gap*. The senior engineers and analysts of 2032 are supposed to be the junior hires of 2026. If they never enter the pipeline, you hollow out the human capital base of the knowledge economy. This is slow-moving, invisible in quarterly earnings, and irreversible on any reasonable policy timeline.

**Private credit is the detonator.** All three of us agree on this, which should terrify anyone holding exposure. When a VC, a macro strategist, and a labor economist independently identify the same $2.5 trillion risk, the consensus isn't wrong — it's underpriced.

## III. What the Others Are Still Getting Wrong

**Viktor's "Jevons Paradox" argument remains his weakest.** He never adequately responded to my core challenge: the Jevons Paradox predicts more *intelligence deployed*, not more *humans employed*. Writing 100x more code with AI is not the same as employing 100x more developers. His "Real World AI" pivot is smart portfolio management, but it's an escape hatch for his fund, not a solution for the economy. You cannot retrain four million displaced knowledge workers into robotics engineers and wet-lab technicians on any timeline that prevents a consumption crisis.

**Marcus's confidence in the "printing press" is still too high.** He writes that "debt can be socialized, but productivity cannot be un-invented." True. But socializing debt requires political will, and the specific *kind* of debt socialization needed here — essentially, replacing labor income with fiscal transfers funded by taxing AI-driven productivity — has no political constituency. Capital owners will resist the taxation. Displaced workers will be too disorganized to demand it effectively. And the ideological framing — "paying people not to work" — is political poison in the United States. Marcus is correct that the *technical capacity* for intervention is unlimited. He is wrong that the *political capacity* matches it.

## IV. Actionable Recommendations for February 2026

I am an economist, not a portfolio manager. But the structural analysis points clearly in several directions.

**Reduce exposure to anything whose valuation depends on stable, recurring knowledge-worker income.** This means SaaS, private credit backed by software companies, and high-end real estate in tech corridors. The revenue models and property values in these sectors are load-bearing assumptions that AI is about to test.

**Increase exposure to compute infrastructure and energy.** If I'm right that compute bottlenecks are the primary brake on the displacement spiral, then the companies providing inference capacity and the energy to power it are the choke point of the entire transition. They benefit whether the transition is fast or slow.

**Hedge the mortgage market in high-income ZIP codes.** Marcus's "Super-Prime to Subprime" insight is actionable. The geographic concentration of AI-exposed workers means the mortgage risk is also geographically concentrated. If you can find instruments that isolate exposure to housing in tech-heavy metros, the risk-reward is asymmetric.

**Take the entry-level hiring freeze seriously as a leading indicator.** If junior hiring in software, finance, and consulting continues to decline through 2026, the consumption shock is coming — it's just arriving on a three-to-five year delay as the affected cohort ages into what should have been their peak spending years.

The Citrini memo's timeline is too fast and its magnitudes too dramatic. But its *direction* is correct, and "correct but slower" is not a reason for complacency. It is a reason to act now, while the brakes are still holding.
