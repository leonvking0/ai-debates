## Mind Changes
- `(Q1)` I have raised my bar for what counts as a "credible exit path." It is not enough that an L1 escape hatch exists in theory. An average user needs a wallet-native emergency mode that can locally construct and sign the L1 exit, switch across multiple RPCs or a light-client path, and broadcast through redundant relays or public mempools. There also need to be mirrored open-source front ends and standardized one-tap forced-withdraw flows. If escape requires a power user playbook, it is not a real guarantee.
- `(Q2)` I am less tolerant of vague "progressive decentralization" promises. My `claim-3` only survives in a narrower form: decentralization has to be scheduled and enforceable. That means sequencer and prover milestones tied to token unlocks, foundation vesting, fee-share release, wallet default placement, exchange support, and institutional collateral eligibility. If an L2 misses those milestones, it should lose distribution and trust, not just take Twitter heat.
- `(Q3)` I also accept more of Gemini’s warning on super-app opacity. Heavy abstraction is only acceptable if the user remains in control underneath it. The safeguards have to be concrete: self-custodial smart accounts, on-chain settlement and data availability, open-source intent and routing policies, signed execution receipts, multi-provider relaying, and one-click provider portability. If a super-app can trap assets, hide execution quality, or become the only usable gateway, then yes, we have recreated fintech with better branding.

## Strongest Remaining Claim
- `claim-2`: Decentralization should be concentrated where failure is catastrophic: custody, censorship resistance, settlement, and user exits. Moderator questions 1 and 3 actually strengthen this. The answer is not to decentralize every UX surface equally; it is to make the trust boundary explicit and enforceable. For the next 560 to 650 million users, the industry needs products that feel like modern apps but fail like crypto should fail: users keep their assets, can verify state, can switch providers, and can exit without asking permission. That is the hard floor. Above that floor, teams should absolutely compete on recovery, gas abstraction, speed, and convenience, because those are the features that stop ordinary users from losing money or abandoning the product.

## Others' Blind Spots
- Gemini is still too willing to let architecture purity dominate product reality. If the system is technically impeccable but onboarding, recovery, and daily execution are worse than a neobank, users will not adopt it at meaningful scale. No users means no real-world sovereignty.
- Gemini also compresses too many different risks into one word, "centralization." A centralized sequencer, a custodial wallet, a single-RPC dependency, and an opaque intent broker are not the same failure mode. Product strategy gets better when we rank them by blast radius and fix the worst ones first.
- My own camp’s blind spot is assuming the market will naturally reward better decentralization over time. Usually it will not. That is why moderator question 2 matters: unless decentralization is tied to economics, distribution, and protocol governance, the profit motive will keep successful systems more centralized than they claim.
