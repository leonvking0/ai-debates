# Memo: The Friction of Reality vs. The Velocity of Theory

**To:** Investment Committee, limited partners, and industry peers
**From:** Viktor Petrov, Managing Partner
**Date:** February 23, 2026
**Subject:** Re: CitriniResearch "2028 Global Intelligence Crisis"

I read the Citrini note this morning. It is, without a doubt, the most coherent articulation of the "deflationary death spiral" thesis I’ve seen. It’s the bear case that keeps me up at night—not because I think it’s 100% probable, but because the mechanism is technically sound even if the sociological assumptions are flawed.

We are sitting here in early 2026. The NASDAQ is effectively pricing in a utopia where margins expand indefinitely because labor costs go to zero. Citrini is pricing in a dystopia where revenue goes to zero because consumers—who are also laborers—lose their purchasing power.

The market believes AI is a printing press. Citrini argues it’s a vacuum cleaner sucking liquidity out of the real economy.

Here is my take, grounded not in macroeconomic theory, but in what I see in the boardrooms of the forty companies we’ve funded.

### 1. The Most Important Question

The single most critical question this scenario raises is not about technology, but about **velocity**.

*Can the real economy of contracts, regulations, and human behavior adapt fast enough to absorb the shock of zero-marginal-cost intelligence?*

Citrini assumes a frictionless transmission mechanism: AI gets better $\rightarrow$ Company fires workers $\rightarrow$ Spending drops $\rightarrow$ SaaS churns $\rightarrow$ Credit defaults.

In a spreadsheet, this happens instantly. In the real world, there is massive **latency**. This latency is our safety buffer. Enterprise procurement cycles take 9 months. Union negotiations take years. Regulatory bodies like the EU or the FTC don't just watch; they intervene. The "friction" that Citrini argues is being destroyed by agents is actually much stickier than tech-optimists (or tech-pessimists) realize.

The question isn't *if* AI can do the work. The question is *if* society will let it happen fast enough to break the bond market before we figure out a new social contract.

### 2. The Deployment Framework

My framework for analyzing this is simple: **Technical Capability $\neq$ Commercial Adoption.**

I spent five years shipping models. I know that the gap between a demo and a deployed, reliable enterprise workflow is often 18 to 24 months.

**The "SaaS Apocalypse" is Real, but Slower**
Citrini is right about one thing: the seat-based SaaS model is dead walking. We are already advising our portfolio companies to pivot to usage-based or outcome-based pricing. If an agent does the work of three SDRs (Sales Development Representatives), you can’t charge for one seat. You have to charge for the meetings booked.

However, the idea that enterprises will cut 15% of their workforce *next quarter* because `Claude Code` is good is naive. Large organizations are fundamentally conservative. They are currently using AI to *augment* staff, not replace them. Why? Because liability. When an autonomous agent hallucinates a discount or leaks PII (Personally Identifiable Information), who goes to jail? Until we have perfect indemnification frameworks, humans stay in the loop. This slows the displacement curve significantly.

**The Jevons Paradox of Intelligence**
The report ignores the supply-side response. When the cost of intelligence drops to near-zero, demand for intelligent products will explode. We aren't just going to use AI to write the same amount of code with fewer people; we are going to write 100x more code.

We are seeing startups build personalized software for individual SMBs—a market that was previously unserviceable. We are seeing AI-driven biotech research that requires *more* wet-lab technicians, not fewer. The economy is not a fixed pie. The "Ghost GDP" concept is clever, but it assumes that the value created by AI stays trapped in the cloud. History shows capital always chases yield. If digital markets saturate, that capital will flood into the physical world—robotics, energy, construction—creating jobs there.

**Intermediation Moats: The Kill Zone**
I agree with the report on "habitual intermediation." If you are a middleman whose only value is a slightly better UI or aggregating search results (Expedia, aggregators, basic insurance brokers), you are in the kill zone. Agents *will* bypass you. This value destruction is real and imminent. But this is "creative destruction," not systemic collapse. The value shifts to the protocol layer or the physical fulfillment layer.

### 3. Confidence Assessment

Here is where I would place my bets, calibrated against the Citrini timeline.

**High Confidence: The Private Credit Crunch (80%)**
This is the strongest part of the bear case. We have billions of dollars in private credit and PE-backed software deals valued at 10x-15x ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue). Those valuations assume low churn and high retention.
If AI agents start aggressively cancelling unused subscriptions or negotiating rates down (which we are already building tools to do), the LTV (Lifetime Value) of a SaaS customer collapses.
Zendesk defaulting is a specific prediction, but the *class* of failure is correct. The "permanent capital" sitting in insurance companies chasing yield in private credit is a systemic bomb. If SaaS cash flows become volatile, that debt gets repriced violently. I am hedging our exposure here.

**Medium Confidence: The White-Collar Wipeout (50%)**
I believe the *tasks* will be automated. I am less confident that the *jobs* will vanish as quickly as 2027-2028.
Employment is sticky. Companies hoard talent. Also, let’s be honest: a lot of "work" in large corps is signaling and politics. AI doesn't solve politics.
However, the entry-level market is broken. We are already seeing it. Why hire a junior analyst when an agent is faster and makes fewer formatting errors? The "ladder" is missing its bottom rungs. This creates a long-term societal crisis, but perhaps not an immediate consumption shock of the magnitude Citrini predicts.

**Low Confidence: The "No Circuit Breakers" Assumption (20%)**
The scenario assumes the Federal Reserve and Treasury sit on their hands while tax receipts drop 12% and the S&P crashes to 3500.
We live in a post-2008, post-2020 world. If "Ghost GDP" becomes real—if corporate profits soar while labor share collapses—the government will simply tax the compute. We will see a "Robot Tax" or a VAT on inference long before we see mass starvation.
The political reaction function is faster than the Citrini timeline suggests. Populism is already high. If AI starts eating the middle class, the regulatory hammer will fall so hard it will make the EU AI Act look like a suggestion box.

### Conclusion

The Citrini memo is a vital stress test. It correctly identifies that **intelligence is a deflationary force**.

But as an investor, I bet on adaptation. The transition will be messy. The SaaS index will likely underperform. The "safe" 10% returns in private credit are likely a mirage. But the idea that we will invent the most powerful tool in human history and use it to impoverish ourselves contradicts the history of capitalism.

We are pivoting our fund away from "Better SaaS" and toward "Real World AI"—defense, manufacturing, bio-pharma, and energy. If the digital world deflates, value flees to the physical. That is the hedge.
